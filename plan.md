Fan Wang — Senior Product Designer Positioning (2025)

## 1. Portfolio Goals

### What a strong senior portfolio communicates in 2025

A senior product designer portfolio does not demonstrate craft — it demonstrates judgment.

Hiring managers for senior roles at fintech companies, AI SaaS startups, and enterprise platforms are not asking "can this person make screens?" They are asking:

- Can this person define the right problem?
- Have they navigated real constraints, not hypothetical ones?
- Did they influence the direction of a product, or just execute a brief?
- Do their design decisions hold up when you ask "why did you choose that over the alternative?"
- Can this person work at the system level — not just the component level?

The portfolio must answer all five questions without the reader having to ask.

### What hiring managers at senior levels actually look for

**In the first 90 seconds:** A clear point of view. Not "I do UX design." Something like: "I design for high-stakes decisions — places where the wrong answer has real consequences." That framing should be visible on the home page and consistent through every case study.

**In the first case study:** Evidence of constraints. Senior work is hard because of what you couldn't do, not because of what you did. Showing constraints immediately signals maturity.

**In the outcome section:** Specificity. "Improved satisfaction" is worthless. "Reduced support escalations by 30% in the first 60 days" is a conversation starter.

**Across the full portfolio:** Variation. If every case study has the same structure, the reader concludes you have one mode. Senior designers adapt to context.

## 2. Current Gaps

### Critical gaps, by order of impact

**1. OLG case study has zero real images.**
The entire OLG page — your strongest case study structurally — is a series of gray placeholder boxes. This is the first impression of a password-protected case study that a hiring manager has to actively unlock. It needs to show real work, not "Replace with hero image."

**2. No before/after evidence.**
None of the three case studies shows a before state. You describe transformation (OLG: content sprawl → clear structure; Pfizer: fragmented tools → unified platform) but never prove it visually or structurally. This is the single biggest missed opportunity. Before/after is the most visceral demonstration of impact available to a designer.

**3. Hero sections feel like a template.**
Pfizer and Intuit both open with a rounded card box containing "Product / Problem / Role / Focus" in a 4-column DL grid. This pattern signals "I used a template" before the reader has read a single word. The structure is fine; the visual treatment needs to be distinct per case study.

**4. No decision rationale anywhere.**
Every case study shows what was built. None of them shows what was considered and rejected. Hiring managers at senior levels specifically probe for this. "Why did you choose intent-based navigation over role-based navigation?" If that question isn't answered in the case study, you're leaving it to chance in the interview.

**5. Intuit has no quantified outcomes.**
"Reduced ambiguity" and "clearer reasoning" are soft outcomes. Intuit is your AI story — the strongest differentiator in your portfolio for fintech/AI roles. It needs at least one number. Even a qualitative proxy ("8/10 users in testing could identify the correct income category on first attempt vs. 4/10 baseline") is stronger than pillars with no data.

**6. The three case studies are structurally similar despite the goal of differentiation.**
All three follow: hero card → problem → research → solution → outcomes. The section labels differ but the rhythm is the same. Differentiation should be felt in the reading experience, not just described in CLAUDE.md.

**7. About page has emoji in section headings.**
"How I work ⚙️" and "How I got here ✨" signal casual/junior energy. The personal narrative on the About page is actually your strongest asset (finance background → design because systems were the problem, not people). That story needs to be told with editorial weight, not emoji.

**8. The "Things I love" section competes with professional content for limited page space.**
LEGO and latte art are fine. They should not be a primary section in the same visual weight as your design philosophy. Move it to a sidebar or reduce to one line.

**9. Home page hero framing is slightly junior.**
"Hello, thanks for dropping by…" with a typewriter effect is charming. It is not senior. The headline "complex systems feel simple" is strong. The typewriter intro undercuts it.

**10. No explicit cross-functional or stakeholder influence narrative.**
Senior designers are expected to have moved decisions, not just executed them. The OLG case study mentions stakeholder alignment but does not tell the story of a specific decision you influenced. "I ran workshops" is table stakes. "Here is the specific disagreement I resolved, and here is how I resolved it using evidence" is what senior means.

### Phase 1 — Structure

**Goal:** Fix the foundational issues that undermine credibility before the work is even read.

#### 1.1 Add real images to OLG

The OLG case study cannot ship with placeholder boxes. Priority order:
- Hero image (actual cover or a representative IA diagram)
- Before/after sitemap or navigation comparison
- Workshop artifact or audit output snapshot
- Final design or key page example

If NDA prevents showing screens, create abstracted diagrams. A well-labeled IA map is more impressive than a redacted screenshot.

#### 1.2 Build a before/after section for each case study

This is the highest ROI change available.

**OLG:** Show the original navigation structure vs. the new one. Even a simplified site map comparison works. The key framing: "This is what we inherited. This is what we changed. Here's why."

**Pfizer:** Show what "fragmented tools" actually looked like. A simplified system diagram showing 3–4 disconnected tools → 1 unified platform is clearer and more impressive than bullet points describing fragmentation.

**Intuit:** Show the failure mode first. What does a user do when the current tax filing system surfaces an ambiguous income question? Show the moment of failure, then show your solution.

#### 1.3 Fix hero sections — differentiate them visually

- **OLG:** Keep the current editorial/light treatment. It works. Remove the CaseStudyHero wrapper and let the headline breathe into the page without a bounding box.
- **Pfizer:** The boxed hero card (white, rounded, bordered) should be dissolved into the page. Let the purple accent and the platform framing do the work.
- **Intuit:** The hero is the strongest of the three (centered layout, blue glow orbs). Lean into it further — the AI framing should feel different from the start.

Remove the shared "Product / Problem / Role / Focus" 4-column DL grid from both Pfizer and Intuit heroes. It is too visually similar to OLG's executive summary pattern. Replace it with a single strong framing sentence per case study:

- Pfizer: "Three disconnected tools replaced by one platform — designed around how healthcare professionals actually learn."
- Intuit: "Most tax software asks users to interpret rules. This one interprets them for users."

#### 1.4 About page — remove emoji from section headings

Change "How I work ⚙️" → "How I work" and "How I got here ✨" → "How I got here." Promote the personal narrative to a primary typographic treatment. Reduce "Things I love" to a single inline sentence or move it below the fold.

#### 1.5 Home page — reconsider the typewriter intro

Replace "Hello, thanks for dropping by…" with a line that establishes your point of view immediately. Options:
- "I design for the moments when the wrong decision has real consequences."
- "I design decision systems — for enterprise platforms, regulated products, and AI workflows."
- Remove entirely and let the headline stand alone.

The headline ("Product designer who makes complex systems feel simple") is strong. The typewriter intro is decorative noise before it.

### Phase 2 — Case Study Improvements

**Goal:** Each case study should feel like a different type of senior design work, not a repeated template.

#### 2.1 OLG — Reframe as governance + system design leadership

**Current framing:** "I redesigned the corporate website."
**Better framing:** "I rebuilt the system by which a regulated public organization decides what to publish, how to structure it, and who owns it."

This is not a UX problem. It is an organizational design problem with UX output. Make that explicit.

**What to add:**
- A stakeholder conflict story. Which two teams wanted incompatible things? How did you resolve it using evidence rather than authority?
- The governance system itself. What decisions did the new structure make easier? What does a content owner do differently now?
- Decision rationale. Why intent-based navigation over role-based navigation? What alternatives did you consider?

**What to rewrite:**
- The "What I led" section is a list of activities. Rewrite as: "I was responsible for the outcome, not just the process. Here is the specific call I made and why."
- The outcome metrics (25% page reduction, 5% retention increase) need sourcing context. How were they measured? Over what period?

**Visual structure:** Keep the current layout — it is the strongest of the three. Add the before/after sitemap comparison and a stakeholder alignment artifact.

#### 2.2 Pfizer — Reposition as platform architecture + compliance UX

**Current framing:** "A unified learning platform."
**Better framing:** "A platform designed for three incompatible needs — learner progress, program management, and compliance certification — without making any of them feel like an afterthought."

The Pfizer case study lists three roles but does not show the design tension between them. A program manager's need for bulk assignment conflicts with a learner's need for clarity. An admin's need for audit-ready reporting conflicts with the desire for a clean interface. Naming these tensions is more impressive than listing features.

**What to add:**
- The role hierarchy diagram already exists (`/work/pfizer/ia.png`) — emphasize it more. This is the intellectual core of the case study.
- One explicit design tradeoff: "We chose [X] over [Y] because of the compliance requirement [Z]."
- A "What scales" section: show which decisions hold as the platform grows to 10x users or 3x programs.

**What to rewrite:**
- The problem section is three bullet points. Each one ("Fragmented systems," "Limited progress visibility," "Weak compliance tooling") deserves a sentence of specific context.
- The outcomes section is entirely non-quantified. Replace with at least one number — even from pilot testing.
- The reflection ("Enterprise design is system design — not just screens") is a good insight buried in two short sentences. Expand it into a 3–4 sentence point of view.

**Visual structure:** Dissolve the boxed hero. Let the section structure breathe. Use the IA diagram as the centerpiece of the architecture section, not a supporting figure.

#### 2.3 Intuit — Strengthen as AI UX + decision design

**Current framing:** "An AI agent for ambiguous tax decisions."
**Better framing:** "Most AI products shift interpretive burden to the user. This one removes it."

The "This was not a chatbot problem — it was a system behavior problem" section is your strongest single piece of writing in the entire portfolio. It needs to be closer to the top, not buried near the end.

**What to add:**
- A decision model diagram that shows: User states situation → System identifies gaps → System asks targeted questions → System generates recommendation → User reviews reasoning → User confirms. This is the core of the work. It should be a visual, not a paragraph.
- One failure mode: What happens when the user's situation is genuinely ambiguous and the system cannot resolve it? How did you design for graceful degradation? This question will be asked in every AI role interview.
- Quantified outcome proxy: Even "In prototype testing, users who went through the AI clarification flow selected the correct income category 2.1× more often than those who used the current form" is enough.

**What to rewrite:**
- The outcome section pillars ("Reduced Ambiguity," "Clearer Reasoning," "Better Decision Confidence") are vague. Rewrite each with a specific user behavior change.
- The "What I learned" section is thin. Expand to: "The hardest design problem was X. My initial approach was Y. It didn't work because Z. I changed to W and here is why."

**Visual structure:** Keep the layered structure (System Design → Interaction Design → Interface Design). Move "This was not a chatbot problem" to before the interface section. It reframes everything that follows.

### Phase 3 — Differentiation

**Goal:** Add one or two elements that are not in any other portfolio at your level.

#### 3.1 Decision log section (per case study)

At the bottom of each case study, add a "Decisions I made" block — 3–4 rows, formatted as:
- Decision: [what you chose]
- Alternative considered: [what you didn't choose]
- Reason: [why]

Example for OLG:
> **Decision:** Intent-based navigation
> **Alternative:** Role-based navigation (separate sections per audience)
> **Reason:** Analytics showed users didn't know which audience they belonged to — they knew what task they wanted to complete.

This pattern is rare in portfolios. It demonstrates exactly the senior judgment hiring managers probe for in interviews.

#### 3.2 Add a "constraints map" to each case study

Before the solution, show a simple visual of the constraints and how they create tension. Not a table — a sparse diagram showing which constraints pull against each other.

For Pfizer: Compliance rigor ←→ Learner simplicity ←→ Admin control.
For OLG: Stakeholder ownership ←→ Content governance ←→ Audience findability.
For Intuit: AI automation ←→ User trust ←→ Regulatory accuracy.

This is a $0 change that signals systems thinking immediately.

#### 3.3 "What I'd do differently" (genuine, not safe)

Current reflection sections say things like "I'd build more governance documentation." That's safe. Replace with something specific and honest:

> "I over-indexed on the IA structure and under-indexed on helping content owners understand why the new model made their jobs easier. The technical architecture was right. The change management was incomplete."

Safe reflections signal you are performing introspection, not doing it. Genuine ones signal maturity.

#### 3.4 Surface the finance → design story more prominently

The About page says: "I started my career in finance. Working with clients, I kept seeing the same problem: people weren't making bad decisions — they were navigating confusing systems."

This is the most senior-sounding line in the entire portfolio. It should not be buried on the About page behind a nav click. Put a version of it on the home page. It is your differentiator — a designer who understands financial systems from inside the decision-making context, not from outside.

### Phase 4 — Polish

**Goal:** Consistency, refinement, and details that signal production quality.

#### 4.1 Typographic consistency across case studies

Currently:
- OLG uses `#4F46E5` (indigo) as accent
- Pfizer uses `#562499` (purple) as accent
- Intuit uses `#236CFF` (blue) as accent

The per-study accent color system is intentional and good. But heading scale, body size, and label treatment differ slightly across the three. Unify the typographic scale — the per-study color is the differentiation, not the type treatment.

#### 4.2 Image quality and framing

- All images on Pfizer and Intuit should have consistent sizing and rounding. Currently mixing `rounded-2xl` and `rounded-3xl` between studies.
- Captions are inconsistently styled — some are `text-xs`, some `text-sm`. Standardize to `text-xs leading-relaxed text-neutral-600`.
- Add alt text reviews — a few alts are generic ("Pfizer dashboard screen") and could be more descriptive for accessibility.

#### 4.3 Mobile reading experience

The TOC sidebar in OLG disappears on mobile (`hidden lg:block`). On mobile, the section structure has no wayfinding. Consider a compact section indicator at the top of the mobile scroll (current section name, sticky) to preserve orientation.

#### 4.4 Reading progress

OLG has a reading progress bar. Pfizer and Intuit do not. Either add it to all three or remove it from OLG — inconsistency across case studies breaks the "each feels distinct but belongs together" goal.

#### 4.5 Transition into case studies from the work page

Currently the work page cards link directly to locked case studies. Consider adding a one-line outcome callout visible on the card before clicking in. Something that answers "why should I care enough to unlock this?" before asking for a password.
